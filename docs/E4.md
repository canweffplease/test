# E4: transport scheduling (fair share vs SRPT vs EDF) for KV transfers

**Question.** When KV cache transfers compete for the same receiver, does
ordering them by size (SRPT) or by deadline (EDF) beat FIFO / fair share,
and why?

**Short answer.** Ordering by size helps the typical request: SRPT cuts the
median request's distance from floor from 4.05x to 2.59x at 70 % load and
from 6.85x to 2.85x at 90 %. It also lowers mean completion time by 3-6 %
at 50 % load, 8-33 % at 70 % and 29-39 % at 90 %. It does this by starving
the longest prompts: at 90 % load the p99 of requests with 2.7K+ tokens is
1.6-4.6x worse than under fair share, and Jain's index drops from 0.81 to
0.57. EDF with the default 2x slack improves the median a little but has
the worst tail of all (p99 33x at 70 %, 46x at 90 %). Once the receiver is
backlogged, EDF degenerates toward arrival order, and small requests queue
behind everyone whose deadline has already passed. A looser deadline (4x)
moves EDF toward SRPT and gives the best p99 at 50 %.

**All of these gains are far smaller than theory predicts.** EQDS's
receiver priority is soft (one urgent flow gets about 39 % of the link, not
100 %). An upstream pull-pacer bug also hands a finished flow about its own
size in wasted credit afterwards, so a request arriving shortly after
another finished runs at half rate. Together they put a wall at about 2.4x
floor under every policy (figure (b)). Only 1-23 % of requests get below 2x.
In the fluid model on the same arrivals, ideal SRPT gives a median of 1.00x.
Both mechanisms are documented below with code citations and micro-tests.

Everything here is EQDS on the 16-node default fat tree. `htsim_roce` and
`htsim_hpcc` ignore `prio` (HTSIM/OUR_CHANGES.md, "Transport scheduling").

deployment each of the 8 decode GPUs sees exactly this pattern on its own
rail. The fabric is non-blocking, so the rails do not interact, and the
per-request slowest-shard rule reduces to the single shard. This is the same

## Why: theory first

**Fair share vs SRPT for flows that start together.** Take N flows on one
link of capacity C with sizes s_1 <= ... <= s_N. Processor sharing finishes
the k-th smallest at

    T_k(PS)   = ( s_1 + ... + s_{k-1} + (N - k + 1) s_k ) / C

so every flow waits for an equal share of everyone still present, and small
flows wait the longest relative to their size. SRPT serves them one at a
time:

    T_k(SRPT) = ( s_1 + ... + s_k ) / C

Both finish the last flow at sum(s)/C (work conservation), but SRPT
minimizes the sum and mean of completion times (Schrage 1968). For
the k-th flow, SRPT replaces (N - k + 1) s_k with s_k, so the gain is
largest for the smallest flows. The largest flow gets nothing back.
With Poisson arrivals (M/G/1) a preemptive policy also lets later small
arrivals overtake. Under heavy-tailed sizes most requests prefer SRPT to PS,
but the very largest can do worse at high load (Bansal and Harchol-Balter,
SIGMETRICS 2001). The fluid model shows exactly that on our arrivals:
ideal SRPT gives median 1.00x at every load, but the largest request's x
goes from 8.5 (PS) to 22.4 at 90 %.

**Static size is nearly as good as remaining size here.** `prio = bytes` is
fixed per flow, so a 4K-token flow that is 99 % done still ranks behind a
fresh 3K-token one. The fluid model quantifies the cost: SJF (static) vs
SRPT (remaining) differ by <= 0.2 in mean x. Only the tail grows (p99
14.8 vs 8.1 at 90 %), because nearly finished long flows get preempted.

**EDF interpolates between FCFS and SJF.** With deadline = a_i + k x f_i
(arrival plus k times the floor), EDF serves i before j iff
a_i + k f_i < a_j + k f_j. As k -> 0 that is arrival order (FCFS). As k ->
infinity it is shortest-first (static SJF). The slack is therefore not just
a scoring threshold but the scheduling policy itself. The fluid model shows
the progression at 90 %: median 6.3 (FCFS), 3.9 (k = 1.5), 3.7 (k = 2),
2.0 (k = 4), 1.0 (SJF). EDF is optimal for meeting deadlines only when all
of them can be met. Under overload every waiting request's deadline is
already past, so EDF serves them oldest-deadline first. A new small request
then queues behind the whole backlog: the head-of-line blocking FCFS is
known for, and the "domino effect" D3 (Wilson et al., SIGCOMM 2011) argues
against. That is why EDF 2x/1.5x have worse tails than plain fair share at
70-90 %, both ideal and measured. EDF hurts *short* requests (short p50
8.2x vs long 4.7x at 90 %), the opposite of SRPT.

## Why: how EQDS orders credit (code)

EQDS is receiver driven. A sender gets one speculative window for free, then
sends only against credit ("pulls") from the receiver.

- **One pull pacer per receiving host**: `main_eqds.cpp:550` creates
  `EqdsPullPacer` per node, and every `EqdsSink` into that host shares it.
  The pacer emits one pull per MTU time at the host line rate:
  `_pktTime` in the constructor, `eqds.cpp:2173-2181`, re-armed at
  `eqds.cpp:2264`. Each pull is worth `_credit_per_pull` = 8 quanta x 512 B
  = 4 KiB (`eqds.cpp:23`, `eqdspacket.h:30`), so credit is allocated one
  packet at a time.
- **Three queues, served in a fixed order** (`EqdsPullPacer::doNextEvent`,
  `eqds.cpp:2183`): `_rtx_senders` first (`2198-2213`), then
  `_active_senders` (`2214-2234`), then `_idle_senders` (`2235-2257`,
  speculative "slow pulls"). Declarations and comments: `eqds.h:517-523`.
- **Where priority acts**: `EqdsPullPacer::mostUrgent` (`eqds.cpp:2267-2280`)
  scans the list and returns the first sink with the lowest
  `EqdsSink::priority()`. It is applied to `_rtx_senders` (`2199`) and to
  `_active_senders` (`2215`). A served sink is re-queued at the back
  (`2209`, `2228`), so equal priorities stay round robin, which is how
  "fifo" (all prio 2,000,000) becomes fair share. `_idle_senders` stays
  plain FIFO. The priority itself is `EqdsSink::_priority`
  (`eqds.h:416-424`, `477`), set from the matrix at `main_eqds.cpp:620-621`.
- **How a sink enters the active list**: data packets carry the source's
  pull target. `EqdsSink::processData` calls `handlePullTarget`
  (`eqds.cpp:1765`), and when the target advances and the sink is not
  already queued it calls `requestPull` (`eqds.cpp:1725-1740`, push at
  `2313`). The sink's demand is `backlog() = pull_target - latest_pull`
  (`eqds.h:401-405`). When a pull leaves it at 0, the sink moves to
  `_idle_senders` (`eqds.cpp:2229-2233`).
- **The source caps its outstanding demand at one window**:
  `computePullTarget` (`eqds.cpp:1054-1069`) limits the target to
  `_maxwnd` minus the credit already held. `handlePull` caps credit at
  `_maxwnd` (`eqds.cpp:614-621`). `_maxwnd` = `-cwnd` x packet size =
  64 x 4,150 B = 265.6 KB by default (`main_eqds.cpp:33, 46, 573-574`).

So this is **receiver-side scheduling**. The switches stay FIFO queues with
trimming and know nothing about priority. What the priority controls is
which sender the receiver lets into its own downlink next.

### Finding 1: priority is soft, and one urgent flow gets ~39 % of the link

`mostUrgent` only chooses among sinks that are in `_active_senders` at the
instant of a pull. In steady state a sink's backlog is refilled one packet
at a time, as each of its data packets arrives with an advanced pull target
(`1765` -> `1725`), and the next pull drains it to 0. In the pull-pacer trace
below, 3,843 of 3,907 active pulls to a flow left its backlog at exactly 0.
The urgent sink then drops out of the active list until its next packet
lands, and the pacer gives the slot to a lower-priority sink. The urgent
flow's pull rate is therefore self-clocked by its own share of packets in
flight. Priority only breaks ties when two sinks have demand in the same
slot, which nudges the shares rather than preempting.

Micro-tests (`scripts/e4.sh diag`, `results/e4/diag/diag_summary.txt`), all
flows into host 8, start at 0, 41.9 MB "small" (floor 839 us), 167.8 MB
"large":

| test | small flow(s) finish | share of link | note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 small + 3 large, no prio | 3,461 us | 24 % | fair share, 1/4 |
| 1 small (prio 1) + 3 large (prio 9) | 2,148 us | 39 % | strict priority would give ~100 %, ~870 us |
| 2 small (prio 1) + 2 large (prio 9) | 2,149 / 2,150 us | 39 % each, 78 % together | |
| 4 small, prio 1/2/3/4 | 2,147 / 2,150 / 3,068 / 4,018 us | | prio 1 and prio 2 finish **together** |
| test_prio: 4 small + 4 large (OUR_CHANGES.md) | 3,901 us | 86 % together | why the original validation looked strict |

With `-cwnd` raised (diagnostic only, direct binary call: run.sh has no
`-cwnd`), the single urgent flow finishes at 2,148 / 2,039 / 1,873 /
1,172 us for cwnd 64 / 128 / 200 / 400 packets. A larger window lets the

only 72 %. The class-level behavior validated in OUR_CHANGES.md (several
urgent flows together take ~all the credit) is real. What fails is
preemption **by a single flow**, and SRPT with distinct sizes always has
exactly one top flow.

### Finding 2: a finished flow keeps receiving credit ("zombie" sink), so the next arrival runs at half rate

Every time an active sink's backlog hits 0 it is `push_back`ed onto
`_idle_senders` (`eqds.cpp:2230`), with no check that it is already there
(`EqdsPullPacer::isIdle`, `eqds.cpp:2298`, exists but is never called).
`requestPull` (`2306-2313`) moves it back to the active list but does not
remove the idle entry. Since nearly every pull drains the backlog
(Finding 1), a sink gains about one stale idle entry per packet it receives.
After the flow finishes, each stale entry is popped and served one slow pull
(`2235-2257`). The re-push guard at `2251-2253` stops the re-push, but
every stale copy still yields one pull. Those pulls go to a source with
nothing to send, which is credit wasted. The idle list is served whenever no
active sink has demand, which with one live flow is every few slots.

Evidence (`results/e4/diag`):

| test (into host 8) | 7.4 MB flow finishes in | x floor |
|---|---|---|
| alone | 174 us | 1.17 |
| 70 us after a 16.2 MB flow finished (`gap70`) | 357 us | 2.41 (half rate) |
| same, but to host 9 (`gap70_otherdst`) | 174 us | 1.17 |
| 200 us after (`gap200`) | 268 us | half rate until ~745 us, then full |
| 300 us / 1,145 us after | 174 us | 1.17 |
| 1,521 us after a **167.8 MB** flow finished (`big_gap1521`) | 357 us | 2.41 |

The effect is per receiver and lasts about as long as the finished flow
took. A `-debug` pull-pacer trace of `gap70`
(`results/e4/diag/gap70_debug_counts.txt`) shows the mechanism directly.
Flow 1 received 3,907 active pulls, 3,843 of which left its backlog at 0.
After it finished it received **3,635 slow pulls, 14.9 MB of credit (92 %
of its own 16.2 MB)**, 2,514 of them while flow 2 was running. Its last slow
pull came at 805 us, with 30,846 quanta of slow credit against a cap of 518.

In the E4 runs this shows up as receiver busy-period efficiency (bytes
delivered / (400 Gbps x time with at least one unfinished flow), column
`busy_eff` in `e4_runs.csv`): 0.71-0.81 at 50 % load, 0.80-0.90 at 70 %,
0.90-0.97 at 90 %. Efficiency rises with load because with many active
sinks the idle list is rarely reached. The ideal would be ~0.99 (4,096 B
payload per 4,150 B packet).

### Together: the 2.4x wall

A lone 4-40 MB flow achieves 1.17x floor (RTT plus headers, `alone`). Half
rate from a zombie gives 2 x 1.17 = 2.4x. One urgent flow among others gets
~39 %, i.e. 2.56x. That is the step in figure (b) and the floor of the SRPT
band in figure (c). Under SRPT almost every request is either the top flow
among competitors or alone right after a predecessor finished, so SRPT is
pinned at 2.4-2.9x while ideal SRPT is at 1.0x. Fair share also loses the
near-floor requests to the zombie effect: 23 % below 2x at 50 % load vs
60 % below 2x in ideal PS (`1 - miss@2`). Priority runs have even fewer
near-floor requests (11 % at 50 %). Likely reason (not isolated): flows
finish one at a time, so more arrivals land within a predecessor's zombie
period.

Whether "SRPT beats fair share" here is therefore a statement about EQDS's
implementation as much as about SRPT. Ideal SRPT vs ideal PS improves the
median by 1.8x / 2.7x / 4.9x at 50/70/90 %. EQDS delivers 1.06x / 1.56x /
2.40x.

### Retransmission credit ordering is not visible

`_rtx_senders` is served before any active sink (`eqds.cpp:2198`), so in
principle a low-priority flow that lost packets jumps the queue. Trims
create retransmission demand (`requestRetransmit` from the NACK path,
`eqds.cpp:1915`, and on an RTS, `1957`). In these runs it does not happen:
across all 45 runs the maximum is 3 retransmitted packets out of 1.3-2.5 M
(`Rtx:` line, `rtx_pkts` in `e4_runs.csv`), with up to ~100 RTS (timeout
probes) and 0 stale NACKs. The receiver's credit keeps its own downlink
queue under the 500-packet (2 MB) limit. The only uncredited traffic is
each new flow's speculative first window: `_credit_spec = _maxwnd` in
`startFlow` (`eqds.cpp:969`, send loop `994`), 265.6 KB, i.e. 5.3 us at
400 Gbps per arrival. That also ignores priority but is too small to
matter. Retransmission ordering would matter on an oversubscribed core,
where EQDS trims heavily (842 k retransmissions on ft128_8os, OUR_CHANGES.md).

## What this means for the comparison with pFabric (and D3)

pFabric (Alizadeh et al., SIGCOMM 2013) puts the scheduling in the
**switches**. Each packet carries the flow's remaining size. Every port
dequeues the most urgent packet and, when full, drops the least urgent.
Senders blast at line rate with minimal rate control. EQDS here does the
opposite: switches are FIFO, and the only scheduler is the **receiver's**
pull pacer deciding whom to credit next.

- **In this incast experiment the two are equivalent in principle.** The
  only contended resource is the receiver's downlink. Credit is paced at
  that downlink's rate, so the receiver's credit order decides the order
  bytes enter the bottleneck queue. A receiver sees every competing flow,
  just as the ToR port in front of it does. A correct receiver-side SRPT
  should reach the ideal-SRPT numbers above. The measured gap is the two
  implementation effects, not a limit of the receiver-side approach.
- **In the core they are not equivalent.** When flows to *different*
  receivers share a congested uplink (oversubscribed fabrics, E2/E5), each
  receiver's pacer orders only its own flows, and no switch prioritizes
  anything. A short flow to host A cannot preempt a long flow to host B on
  a shared core link. pFabric's per-port priority would. Receiver-side
  priority also cannot drop the least urgent packet when a core queue
  overflows: EQDS trims whatever arrives, and as shown above, retransmission
  credit ignores priority.
- **Remaining size vs static size.** pFabric's priority is remaining bytes,
  updated per packet. Ours is the original flow size, fixed at the matrix,
  which is SJF, not SRPT. The fluid model says this costs little in mean,
  but it raises the p99 (14.8x vs 8.1x at 90 %).
- **D3 / EDF.** D3 allocates rate to meet deadlines and rejects or quenches
  flows that cannot make them. Plain EDF has no admission control, which is
  why its tail explodes once the receiver is backlogged.

## Caveats

- Priority is static per flow, set from the matrix. `prio = bytes` is
  shortest-flow-first, not remaining-size SRPT. True SRPT would need the
  sink to re-rank by remaining bytes.
- Priority is per receiving host and applies to new-data credit only.
  Retransmission pulls, the speculative first window, and idle-list slow
  pulls ignore it (OUR_CHANGES.md).
- Results are for EQDS's default `-cwnd 64` (265.6 KB < the ~680 KB BDP of
  a 12 us cross-pod RTT at 400 Gbps). With a larger window priority is
  stricter (72 % of the link at cwnd 400). That would be a different EQDS
  configuration, needing a run.sh change and re-validation against the
  reference numbers.
- The "zombie" credit is an upstream EQDS pull-pacer bug (duplicate entries
  in `_idle_senders`), not something we introduced. It is not fixed here,
  because this task was not allowed to touch HTSIM/. It affects every EQDS
  result where a receiver goes from one flow to the next: E1/E3/E6 single
  or simultaneous-start matrices barely see it, but any staggered-start
  matrix does. A likely fix is to skip the push at `eqds.cpp:2230` when
  `isIdle(sink)`, or to remove the sink from `_idle_senders` in
  `requestPull`. Re-run E4 after any fix. Expect all policies to move toward
  the fluid model, and SRPT most.
- One rail of TP=8. With the full TP group, all eight rails see identical
  matrices, so the slowest-shard maximum over eight near-identical shards
  is close to this single shard, except for ECMP placement differences in
  the core, which are not the bottleneck here.
- The receiver downlink is the only bottleneck. The comparison says nothing
  about core contention (see the pFabric section).
- Realized load varies by seed (0.69-0.81 at nominal 70 %, up to 1.01 at
  90 %). Medians over 3 seeds, paired across policies.
- Deadline miss rates against 2x are 75-99 % under every policy because of
  the 2.4x wall, so `miss@2` does not discriminate. Use `miss@4` from
  `e4_table.csv`, or EDF 4x's own rate (3 / 25 / 50 %).

## After the credit and priority fixes

Both EQDS mechanisms above are now fixed in the simulator
(`HTSIM/OUR_CHANGES.md (EQDS pull pacer section)`, binary `HTSIM/htsim/sim/build_eqds2`). E4 was
rerun unchanged otherwise: same 45 matrices, same seeds,
`HTSIM_BUILD=build_eqds2 OUTDIR=results/e4_fixed FIGPREFIX=e4_fixed
./scripts/e4.sh run` then `plot`. 45/45 runs complete, every request
finishes, 0 lossless violations, at most 3 retransmitted packets per run,
0 stale NACKs. Results are in `results/e4_fixed/`, figures in
`results/figs/e4_fixed_*`. The sections above describe the old binary and
are kept as the record of the bug.

**What changed in EQDS.**

1. *Zombie credit (Finding 2).* A sink now tells its pull pacer when its
   flow is complete (all payload bytes received). The pacer then drops
   every entry for it from all three lists, including the stale
   `_idle_senders` copies, and never credits it again. The duplicate idle
   entries of a *live* flow are deliberately kept. They over-grant
   speculative credit beyond the one-window cap, and a lone flow needs that
   because `-cwnd 64` (265.6 KB) is below the BDP. Removing them as well
   slows a lone 7.4 MB flow from 1.17x to 1.27x floor and moves the
   reference run from 4,164.7 to 4,217.3 us.
2. *Soft priority (Finding 1).* The pacer now ranks sinks by **uncredited
   bytes** (flow wire bytes minus the speculative first window minus all
   credit granted so far), not by the one-window pull-target demand that
   drains to 0 after every pull. The most urgent live sink with uncredited
   bytes gets the pull whenever it is strictly more urgent than the sink the
   old order would serve. Equal priorities are still round robin.
   Retransmission pulls still go first.

With no `prio` (or every flow at 2,000,000, i.e. "fair share") the code
path is the old one plus fix 1. The reference run
(`kv_llama70b_4k_tp8.cm`, seed 13) and `kv_incast_8to1.cm` give
byte-identical logs: 4,164.7 us and 27,645.3 us
(`results/e4_fixed/regression/`).

### What changed and why

- **The 2.4x wall is gone.** At 70 % load the share of requests in the
  2.3-2.6x band falls from 47 % to 2.7 % under SRPT, from 31 % to 4.1 %
  under EDF, and from 14 % to 4.5 % under fair share (3 seeds pooled,
  n = 444 each; figure `e4_fixed_cdf_load70`). The share finishing below 2x
  rises from 2.3 % to 79 % (SRPT), from 2.3 % to 57 % (EDF) and from 6.5 %
  to 29 % (fair share). Below 1.5x: 69 % under SRPT, against 1.8 % before.
  The wall was the sum of the two mechanisms: half-rate after a zombie and
  ~39 % for a lone urgent flow. Both are gone. The receiver's busy-period
  efficiency is now 0.96-0.97 at every load, against 0.71-0.81 at 50 %
  before. The remaining ~3 % is headers (64 B per 4,160 B packet = 1.5 %)
  plus credit hand-over gaps.
- **SRPT's median now sits on the fluid model.** It is 1.10 / 1.13 / 1.18x
  at 50 / 70 / 90 %, against 2.43 / 2.59 / 2.85x before. Ideal SRPT is
  1.00 / 1.00 / 1.19, ideal SJF (static size, which is what `prio = bytes`
  is) 1.00 / 1.00 / 1.00. The residual ~0.1 is the per-flow cost of a real
  transfer, which a fluid server does not pay: one RTT, the headers, and the
  speculative ramp. A lone 7.4 MB flow is 1.17x. SRPT's mean x falls from
  2.52 / 3.16 / 3.56 to 1.36 / 1.82 / 2.21 (ideal SJF 1.25 / 1.58 / 1.99).
  Ideal SRPT over ideal PS improves the median by 1.8x / 2.7x / 4.9x at
  50 / 70 / 90 %. EQDS now delivers 1.66x / 2.88x / 5.47x, against
  1.06x / 1.56x / 2.40x before. Paired per seed, SRPT has the lower median and
  the lower mean than fair share in 9 of 9 (load, seed) cells.
- **Fair share moves toward ideal PS, but less.** Its median drops from
  2.57 / 4.05 / 6.85 to 1.83 / 3.26 / 6.46 (ideal PS 1.76 / 2.69 / 5.78).
  Fair share gains only from fix 1, because it has no priority. It gains
  most at 50 %, where a receiver most often goes from one flow to the next.
- **Starvation of long prompts is sharper, and now matches the fluid
  model.** Under SRPT the long-prompt tail barely moves in absolute terms:
  long p99 5.2 / 16.5 / 24.6 before, 5.3 / 14.8 / 24.4 after. Long max
  5.6 / 15.4 / 24.6 after, against ideal SJF 5.4 / 12.8 / 23.4. What changes is
  everyone else. Short prompts now finish at ~1.1x, so the gap between
  short and long, and the gap to fair share, widen. Jain's index under
  SRPT falls from 0.929 / 0.719 / 0.570 to **0.816 / 0.471 / 0.311** (ideal
  SJF 0.82 / 0.54 / 0.30). Long p99 is higher than under fair share in 9
  of 9 cells (s3 at 90 %: 54.7x vs 11.2x). SRPT's overall p99 is lower
  than fair share's at 50 % (3/3 seeds), split at 70 % (2/3) and higher at
  90 % (2/3).
- **EDF now shows its theoretical shape, including the bad part.** At 50 %
  load EDF 2x's median drops to 1.12 (ideal 1.00). At 90 % its p99 rises
  from 45.6 to 70.2 and its mean from 10.2 to 13.1 (ideal EDF 2x p99 43.8,
  mean 6.9). With strict priority, EDF really serves overdue requests in
  deadline order, so the domino effect described above is now enforced
  rather than diluted by soft priority. EDF 2x/1.5x hurt short requests
  (short p50 8.1x / 10.4x at 90 %, against long 3.6x), the opposite of SRPT.
  The measured EDF tail exceeds the fluid model's at 90 % because
  realized load there is 0.90-1.01 (seed 3 is overloaded). At ~96.5 %
  efficiency the effective load is 0.93-1.04, and EDF's backlog tail is
  very sensitive to that. EDF 4x still sits between EDF 2x and SRPT, as
  the theory section predicts.

**What still separates EQDS from the fluid model:** the per-flow transfer
overhead (~1.1x at the median), ~3 % efficiency loss, static priority
(SJF, not remaining-size SRPT), and the unprioritized parts of credit
(retransmission pulls, the 265.6 KB speculative first window of every new
flow, idle slow pulls). The caveats above about core contention and the
pFabric comparison are unchanged. With strict receiver priority, the
receiver-side scheduler now behaves like the ideal one in this incast,
which supports "equivalent in principle". Wall clock per run is unchanged
(median 25 s, 14-45 s).
